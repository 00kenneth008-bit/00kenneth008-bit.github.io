Plan a month of content in Notion without losing ideas

5 min read · A practical guide from kenneth008

Every creator has the same problem, and it isn’t a lack of ideas. It is a pile of ideas in five different places — a notes app, a chat message to yourself, a screenshot, a half-written draft — and no idea which ones you already planned, which ones you already published, or which ones were actually good.

Planning a month of content does not require a complicated system. It requires three habits and one place to keep them: capture everything the moment it appears, decide what it becomes once a week, and plan by platform instead of by panic.

Capture everything, the moment it appears

Ideas do not arrive on schedule. They arrive in the shower, on a walk, halfway through somebody else’s video. The rule is simple: capture now, judge later. If capturing takes more than ten seconds, you will skip it, and the idea will be gone by dinner.

Keep one inbox for raw ideas. Not a folder, not a board with twelve columns — one list, where every idea lands in the same form: a title, a sentence about what it is, and where it came from if that matters. A voice memo counts. A photo of a whiteboard counts. The inbox is not the place for finished thinking. It is the place where thinking starts.

The inbox only works if it is the only inbox. The moment ideas also live in your notes app and your chat history, you have three inboxes and no system. Pick one, and put everything there for a month. You can always change it later; what you cannot do is search three places every time you sit down to plan.

Triage once a week, for twenty minutes

Once a week, open the inbox and give every idea one of three decisions:

  • Make it. It has a platform, a format, and a rough slot in the plan. Move it into the plan.
  • Park it. It is interesting but not now. Give it a tag — a topic, a series, a season — and leave it where you can find it later.
  • Drop it. It sounded good for a minute and it isn’t. Delete it. An inbox full of ideas you will never make is just guilt with a search bar.

Twenty minutes is enough, because the decisions are small. The weekly triage is not a strategy meeting. It is a sorting habit. The strategy happens once a month, when you look at what you actually published and what happened to it.

Plan by platform, not by panic

One idea is rarely one piece of content. The same idea can be a long post on one platform, a short video on another, and a thread somewhere else. Planning by platform means deciding, for each idea, where it goes and what shape it takes there — instead of waking up, opening an app, and wondering what to post today.

A month’s plan does not need to be a calendar full of finished titles. It needs to answer three questions for each week: what are we publishing, where, and in what form? If those three are decided, the daily work is making, not deciding. And making is the part you actually enjoy.

Leave gaps on purpose. A plan with no empty days breaks the first time life happens. A plan with two open slots a week absorbs the unexpected — the trend, the reply, the idea that arrived on Tuesday and deserves to go out on Wednesday.

Track what happened, not what you hoped

After a piece is published, log the result. Not a deep analytics review — just the numbers that matter for the next plan: where it went, when, and how it did relative to your other recent pieces. A simple log, kept in the same place as the plan, turns a month of guessing into a month of evidence.

Once a month, read the log before you plan. Which topics got a response? Which formats fell flat? Which platform is actually growing, and which one are you feeding out of habit? The answers are usually obvious once they are written down, and almost impossible to see when they are not.

The point is not to chase numbers. It is to stop repeating the pieces that didn’t work and start repeating the ones that did — on purpose, instead of by accident.

The weekly review is the system

None of this works without the review. A planning system that nobody opens is a template, not a system. The weekly twenty minutes — inbox, triage, next week’s plan — is the habit that makes everything else real. Miss a week and the inbox fills up. Miss two and you are back to five different places.

Put the review in the calendar, same time every week. Treat it like a meeting with yourself that you do not reschedule. It is the cheapest twenty minutes in a creator’s week: it is the difference between a plan and a pile.

Ideas are cheap. Kept ideas, planned and published, are the business. The gap between the two is a list, a weekly twenty minutes, and a log that tells you what to do more of.
